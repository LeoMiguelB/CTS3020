# Post 3: The Voter Suppression Trail — Procedural Rhetoric and Political Corruption

*The Voter Suppression Trail*, a game inspired by *The Oregon Trail*, created by Michael Grynszpan, Chris Baker, and Brian Moore, aims to capture the modern process of casting a ballot on Election Day (Grynszpan et al.). As opposed to simply presenting raw statistics, it uses procedural rhetoric to expose political corruption around voting. By allowing the players to be co-creators, and using ethical digital storytelling to convey the problems around voting, *The Voter Suppression Trail* effectively fights against elite political narratives and exposes how higher-ups take advantage of administrative processes.

As opposed to presenting mundane papers around election laws, *The Voter Suppression Trail* presents it in such a way that it affords agency to the player. The idea of "audience as co-creator" from Bryan Alexander explains how requiring folks to use mental and physical participation to navigate the story helps immerse the audience and is an effective tool to convey a message in a digital story (Alexander). In the game, players start off by selecting a persona whose demographic dictates the difficulty: a white software programmer in California votes within minutes with no friction, while a black voter in Wisconsin or a Latina nurse in Texas has to wait hours and faces numerous obstacles (Grynszpan et al.). Through the game's procedural mechanics, it effectively generates anxiety and exhaustion, which helps the audience understand the feeling of fighting against politically motivated obstacles.

The simulation effectively challenges the institutional power dynamics in politics. Corrupt lawmakers often hide away voter suppression under so-called election integrity. Amanda Hill says that ethical digital storytelling must challenge dominant power dynamics by shifting from "viewing stories as data to viewing them as relational exchanges" (Hill). *The Voter Suppression Trail* achieves this by focusing on the perspectives of the general public rather than the politicians. It highlights the reality of the public and how there are real barriers for some and for others not so much. It exposes the common political strategies of higher-ups and how they are commonly put in place to oppress certain demographics.

Additionally, the game consistently employs the idea of the "danger of a single story" from Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (Adichie). Corrupt political systems often depend on a single narrative: the idea that the election is done equally and is done fairly. *The Voter Suppression Trail* shines light on how false that is by contrasting the difficulty amongst the different personas that can be played; some struggled, and others faced very little to no obstacles. The game portraying different perspectives also follows Hill's principles of responsible storytelling (Hill), and it effectively helps to describe the oppression engineered by politicians who seek to gain power.

Ultimately, *The Voter Suppression Trail* shows the power of these types of games to help confront political corruption. By allowing the audience to be co-creators and using procedural mechanics in relational ethics, the digital story exposes the quiet, hidden patterns that violate the integrity that these higher-ups promised. The game effectively shows how one can use games as a digital story format to shine light on political corruption.
